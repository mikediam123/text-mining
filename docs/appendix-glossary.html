Appendix B — Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the terms used in this book, in alphabetical order. Where a term is explained in a chapter, the chapter is noted.

Accuracy. The share of all predictions that were correct. Misleading when one answer is much more common than the other, because “always guess the common one” scores well (Chapter 7).

Alpha (in LDA). A setting that controls how mixed each document’s topics are. Small values push each document toward one or two topics (Chapter 4).

Anti-join. A way of combining two tables that keeps only the rows of the first table that have no match in the second. We use it to remove stop words (Chapter 2).

AUC (area under the curve). A threshold-free score for how well a model ranks “yes” cases above “no” cases. 0.5 is guessing and 1.0 is perfect (Chapter 7).

Beta (in topic models). For each topic, the probability of each word. The words with the highest beta define what the topic is about (Chapter 4).

Bigram. A pair of neighboring words, such as “financial aid.” A trigram has three words, and in general an n-gram has n (Chapter 2).

Coefficient. A number that says how strongly something pushes a prediction. In a regression, it is the estimated difference in the outcome. In the lasso, most coefficients are zero, and the nonzero ones identify the important words (Chapter 5, Chapter 7).

Confidence interval. A range of values that plausibly contains the true quantity. We draw 95% intervals as error bars: the average plus or minus 1.96 standard errors (Chapter 6).

Confusion matrix. A 2×2 table counting a classifier’s hits, misses, false alarms, and correct rejections (Chapter 7).

Cosine similarity. A score from -1 to 1 for how closely two vectors point in the same direction. For embeddings, high means similar meaning. When vectors are scaled to length 1, it equals their dot product (Chapter 8).

Covariate. A characteristic of a document or person that you want to relate to the outcome, such as a student’s level (Chapter 5).

Cross-validation. Estimating how well a model will do on new data by repeatedly training on part of the training data and testing on the rest. cv.glmnet() uses it to pick the penalty strength (Chapter 7).

Data frame / tibble. A table where each row is an observation and each column is a variable. A tibble is the tidyverse’s version (Appendix A).

Data leakage. Information from the test data accidentally influencing the model, which makes test results look better than they would be on genuinely new data (Chapter 7).

Dimension. One position in a vector. MiniLM’s embeddings have 384 dimensions (Chapter 8).

Document. Whatever unit of text you decide to treat as one piece: an answer, a student, a question. Your choice shapes every result (Chapter 1).

Document-term matrix. A grid with one row per document and one column per word, with word counts in the cells (Chapter 4).

Embedding. A list of numbers that represents a text so that texts with similar meanings get similar numbers. It is like an address on a map of meaning (Chapter 8).

F1. A single score that balances precision and recall. It is low whenever either one is low (Chapter 7).

Factor. A column with a fixed set of allowed categories in a stated order (Chapter 1, Chapter 5).

Few-shot learning. Learning a task from only a small number of labeled examples. Pretrained models often make this possible (Chapter 8).

FREX. A way of ranking a topic’s words by how both frequent and exclusive to the topic they are. Useful for naming topics (Chapter 5).

Function. A named recipe: you give it inputs (arguments) and it returns a result (Appendix A).

Gamma (in topic models). For each document, how much of it belongs to each topic. A document’s gammas sum to 1 (Chapter 4).

IDF (inverse document frequency). A rarity score for a word: high when the word appears in few documents, zero when it appears in all of them (Chapter 3).

K. The number of topics a topic model is asked to find. You choose it (Chapter 4).

Label noise. Errors or disagreement in the human-assigned labels that a supervised model learns from. It limits how well any model can do (Chapter 7).

Lambda. The strength of the penalty in a regularized regression. Chosen by cross-validation (Chapter 7).

Lasso. A regularized regression that shrinks most coefficients all the way to zero, leaving a short list of important words (Chapter 7).

LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation). The most common topic model. It describes each document as a mix of topics and each topic as a list of words (Chapter 4).

Learning curve. A chart of how a model’s performance changes as it is given more training examples. Used to see how many labeled examples a method needs (Chapter 8).

Lexicon. A word list with labels attached, such as which words are positive or negative, or which words are stop words (Chapter 2, Chapter 6).

Lift. How many times more common something is in one group than overall. 1 means no difference; 2 means twice as common (Chapter 4).

Log-likelihood. A score for how well a model fits the data. Higher is better, but it always improves as you add topics, so it cannot choose K by itself (Chapter 4).

Median. The middle value: half the values are smaller and half larger. Less affected by extreme values than the mean (Chapter 1).

Multi-label. A prediction problem where each item can have several labels at once, such as an answer coded with more than one topic (Chapter 7).

Negation. Words like “not” and “never” that flip a meaning. Simple lexicon methods cannot see them (Chapter 6).

Overfitting. A model that learns the quirks of its training data so well that it does poorly on new data (Chapter 7).

Perplexity. A measure of how well a topic model predicts documents it has not seen. Lower is better (Chapter 4).

Pipe (|>). R’s “and then” symbol. It passes what is on its left into the function on its right (Appendix A).

Precision. Of the cases a model flags as “yes,” the share that really are. “When it says yes, can I trust it?” (Chapter 7).

Pretrained model. A model that has already learned from a huge amount of text before you use it, so that you can borrow what it learned instead of starting from nothing (Chapter 8).

Prevalence (in STM). How much of the text belongs to a topic. STM lets prevalence depend on covariates (Chapter 5).

Recall. Of the cases that really are “yes,” the share the model found. “How many did it catch?” (Chapter 7).

Regression. A method for estimating how an outcome changes with something else. The result is a coefficient (Chapter 5).

Regular expression (regex). A compact pattern language for searching text, such as ^\\d+$ for “only digits” (Appendix A).

Regularization. Adding a penalty on the size of a model’s coefficients so it does not overfit (Chapter 7).

Seed. The starting point of R’s random number generator. Setting it makes random steps reproducible (Appendix A).

Sentiment. How positive or negative language is (Chapter 6).

Sparse matrix. A grid that is mostly zeros, stored compactly by recording only the non-zero cells (Chapter 4, Chapter 7).

Standard error. A measure of how uncertain an estimate (such as an average) is. Shrinks as the group gets larger (Chapter 6).

Stemming / lemmatizing. Reducing words to a root or dictionary form, so “advisor,” “advisors,” and “advising” count as one (Chapter 2).

STM (structural topic model). A topic model that lets topic prevalence depend on document characteristics (Chapter 5).

Stop words. Very common function words (“the,” “and,” “of”) removed before counting (Chapter 2).

Supervised learning. Training a model on examples with known answers, then testing it on examples it has not seen (Chapter 7).

TF (term frequency). How common a word is within a document (Chapter 3).

TF-IDF. TF multiplied by IDF. High for words that are frequent in one document and rare in the others (Chapter 3).

Threshold. The cutoff probability above which a model’s prediction becomes “yes.” Changing it trades precision against recall (Chapter 7).

Token. The unit of text you count, usually a word (Chapter 2).

Topic. In a topic model, a list of words that tend to appear together. A topic is not a label; you name it yourself by reading its words (Chapter 4).

Transformer. The kind of neural network behind modern language models. MiniLM is a small one (Chapter 8).

Unsupervised learning. Finding patterns in data without an answer key (Chapter 4).

Vector. A list of numbers. An answer’s embedding is a vector (Chapter 8).

Vocabulary. The set of distinct words a model works with, often after dropping very rare ones (Chapter 4, Chapter 7).

Zero-shot learning. Classifying with no labeled examples, by describing the categories and asking a model how well each text fits them (Chapter 9).
